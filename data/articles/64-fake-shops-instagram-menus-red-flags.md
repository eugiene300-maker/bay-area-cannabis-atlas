# Fake Shops and Instagram Menus: Red Flags for San Jose Shoppers

*Polished reels and DM menus are not city registration. A short checklist keeps South Bay adults on the lawful side of scarce storefronts.*

San Jose’s capped cannabis retail market creates a vacuum that social media loves to fill. Flashy Instagram menus, Telegram price lists, and “same-day” stories can look as professional as a licensed storefront—sometimes more so. For shoppers, the question is not whether the graphics are good. It is whether the seller appears on SJPD’s registered cannabis businesses list and holds the required state licenses.

Start with the city’s own language. SJPD’s Division of Cannabis Regulation states that any cannabis business or delivery service not on the registered list is not operating lawfully under the San Jose Municipal Code. That sentence is the entire anti-fake-shop doctrine in one line. Bookmark the list. Search the business name. Confirm the street address. If the account refuses to publish a physical address, or the address never appears on the city roster, treat the pitch as unlicensed. Screenshots age badly; check the live list on the day you plan to buy.

Red flags cluster quickly once you look for them. Menus that only live in DMs. Prices that undercut every taxed legal shelf by a dramatic margin with no explanation. Pressure to pay in cryptocurrency, gift-card codes, or peer-to-peer apps with no receipt. Drivers who will not show identification tied to a licensed operator. “Pop-up” storefronts in unmarked suites. Claims that a shop is “in the lottery” or “waiting on paperwork” while already selling. None of those patterns prove criminal intent by themselves, but together they describe a marketplace outside the inspectable system California built after Proposition 64.

Licensed retail is imperfect and often expensive. San Jose’s combined sales and use tax environment and local cannabis business taxes stack onto wholesale costs. Scarcity limits competition. Those realities make bargain hunters vulnerable. A too-good eighth is not automatically adulterated, but it is also not automatically tested. Legal products must meet labeling and laboratory standards that illicit supply chains do not guarantee. If a seller cannot show a batch label, a license number, and a city registration match, you are guessing about pesticides, potency, and chain of custody.

Mimicry is another trap. Lookalike logos, similar colorways, and borrowed brand photography appear in unlicensed ads statewide. Cross-check brand authenticity at a registered counter rather than trusting a screenshot. If a “deal” requires you to meet in a parking lot behind a strip mall, you are not at a dispensary—you are in a private sale that may violate commercial licensing rules regardless of adult age. “Gifting” language that quietly expects payment for stickers, memberships, or tips is a known workaround pattern statewide; California’s lawful gift rules require no compensation whatsoever and stay within possession limits between adults 21 and older.

Delivery apps and aggregators add confusion. An app logo is not a municipal registration. Confirm the underlying business on SJPD’s list and whether that specific location is authorized for delivery. San Jose’s FAQ and business-application materials continue to emphasize Notices of Completed Registration and closed ordinary intake for new retail and delivery-only applications. Equity pathways create limited new openings; they are not a free-for-all for every viral account.

What to do instead is dull on purpose. Shop registered addresses. Call the phone number listed on the city’s page if you need to confirm hours. Keep receipts. Report suspected unlicensed storefronts through appropriate city channels rather than arguing in comments. If friends share a menu screenshot, ask whether they checked the roster—not whether the budtender in the story seemed nice. Teach new arrivals to San Jose the same habit: city list first, cart second.

Fake shops thrive on urgency and secrecy. Legal shops thrive on boring paperwork. In a city where storefronts stay scarce by design, that paperwork is the shopper’s best fraud filter. Scroll less. Verify more. Leave with a receipt that points to a real address on San Jose’s list—and treat every glossy menu that cannot clear that bar as entertainment, not a store.
